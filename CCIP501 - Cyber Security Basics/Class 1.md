## SECTION A: Introduction and Basic Concepts of Information Security


Q1. What is security?

Answer: Security is the state of being secure and free from danger or harm, as well as the actions taken to protect someone or something.

Security does not necessarily mean that losses can never occur. Instead, it involves taking precautions so that possible losses are manageable and survivable.

Example: A company installs CCTV cameras, locks its server room, and requires employees to use passwords to protect its resources.

Q2. What is information security (InfoSec)?

Answer: Information security is the protection of information and its critical elements, including the systems and hardware that use, store, process, and transmit that information.

It involves applying policies, education, training, awareness, and technology to protect information assets against threats.

Example: A bank encrypts customer records, restricts access to employees with appropriate permissions, and regularly backs up its databases.

Q3. What is the main purpose of information security?

Answer: To protect information assets from unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, destruction, or disruption while ensuring that authorized users can access information when needed.

Q4. Why is information security important to organizations?

Answer: Every organization, whether public or private, large or small, possesses information that needs protection.

Information security is important because it:

- Protects valuable organizational information.
    
- Reduces the risk of data theft and cyberattacks.
    
- Protects the interests of stakeholders.
    
- Supports business continuity.
    
- Maintains trust in information systems.
    
- Helps organizations fulfill their responsibilities to stakeholders.
    

Q5. Who is responsible for information security in an organization?

Answer: Everyone who interacts with organizational information shares responsibility for its security.

However, senior management establishes the direction of security programs, while information security professionals manage and implement controls.

Q6. What are the main areas included in information security?

Answer: The module identifies three areas:

1. Information security management: Planning, organizing, and overseeing the protection of information.
    
2. Data security: Protecting information against unauthorized access, changes, or destruction.
    
3. Network security: Protecting networks and communications against attacks and unauthorized access.
    

Q7. What are the different types of security mentioned in the module?

Answer:

- Physical security: Protecting physical facilities, equipment, and people.
    
- Personal security: Protecting individuals from harm.
    
- Operations security: Protecting organizational activities and operational information.
    
- Communications security: Protecting information during communication.
    
- National security: Protecting a country's important interests and assets.
    
- Network security: Protecting computer networks and the information transmitted through them.

1. Define information security.
    
2. Explain how computer security evolved into information security.
    
3. Define key terms and critical concepts in information security.
    
4. Describe the security responsibilities of different professionals within an organization.
    

## SECTION B: History and Evolution of Information Security

Slides 4–16

Q9. When did computer security begin?

Answer: Computer security began after the development of the earliest mainframe computers.

During World War II, groups involved in code-breaking developed some of the first modern computers, which required protection.

Q10. What were the main security concerns during the early years of computing?

Answer: Early computer security focused mainly on:

1. Physical theft of computer equipment.
    
2. Espionage involving computer systems or their output.
    
3. Sabotage of equipment.
    
4. Physical protection of computer facilities.
    
5. Classification of sensitive documents.
    

Security at this stage was primarily physical security, rather than the broader information security used today.

Q11. What was the Enigma machine, and why is it important in information security history?

Answer: The Enigma was a German encryption machine used to encode messages during World War II.

It illustrates the importance of cryptography, protecting confidential communications, and code-breaking in the early history of information security.

The module notes that earlier versions were broken by Polish cryptanalysts, while British and American researchers later worked on more complex versions.

Keyword — Cryptography: The practice of protecting information by transforming it into a form that unauthorized parties cannot easily understand.

Q12. What happened during the 1960s that influenced computer security?

Answer:

- Mainframe computers became more widely used during the Cold War.
    
- Computers performed increasingly complex tasks.
    
- The Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) investigated redundant networked communications.
    
- Larry Roberts led the development of ARPANET.
    

These developments introduced new concerns about protecting interconnected systems.

Q13. What is ARPA?

Answer: ARPA stands for Advanced Research Projects Agency.

It was an American research agency involved in studying and developing advanced technologies, including networking technology.

Q14. What is ARPANET?

Answer: ARPANET stands for Advanced Research Projects Agency Network.

It was an early computer network developed through ARPA research and became an important predecessor of the modern Internet.

Q15. Who led the development of ARPANET?

Answer: Larry Roberts.

Q16. What major computer security problems emerged during the 1970s and 1980s?

Answer:

1. Weak password structures and formats.
    
2. Inadequate controls at remote computer sites.
    
3. Unsafe dial-up connections.
    
4. Poor or nonexistent user identification systems.
    
5. Inadequate authorization mechanisms.
    
6. Increased opportunities for unauthorized access.
    

Example: An unauthorized person could potentially access a remote computer system because it lacked effective password protection and user authorization.

Q17. What was RAND Report R-609?

Answer: RAND Report R-609 was a major computer security report authored by Willis H. Ware in 1970.

Its full title was Security Controls for Computer Systems: Report of Defense Science Board Task Force on Computer Security.

The report was not declassified until 1979.

Q18. Why was RAND Report R-609 significant?

Answer: It is regarded in the module as a foundational work in computer security because it emphasized that:

- Security involves more than physical protection.
    
- Organizational management must participate in security.
    
- Formal policies are necessary.
    
- Computer data requires protection against unauthorized access.
    
- Security responsibilities extend across multiple organizational levels.
    

Q19. How did computer security change during the 1970s and 1980s?

Answer: Computer security expanded from mainly protecting physical equipment to protecting data and restricting unauthorized access.

Organizations also began recognizing the importance of security policies, management involvement, and employee responsibilities.

Q20. What is MULTICS?

Answer: MULTICS stands for Multiplexed Information and Computing Service.

It was a mainframe, time-sharing operating system developed in the mid-1960s, with security integrated into its core functions.

Q21. Which organizations developed MULTICS?

Answer:

1. General Electric (GE)
    
2. Bell Laboratories
    
3. Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)
    

Q22. Why was MULTICS important to information security?

Answer: MULTICS was an influential early operating system designed with security integrated into its fundamental functions rather than treated as an afterthought.

It helped shape research into operating system security and influenced the creation of UNIX.

Q23. What is UNIX, and how is it related to MULTICS?

Answer: UNIX is an operating system developed by people who had previously worked on MULTICS.

The module identifies text processing as its original primary purpose.

Q24. How did microprocessors affect computer security in the late 1970s?

Answer: Microprocessors made computing technology more accessible and expanded computing capabilities.

As computer use increased, the number and variety of potential security threats also increased.

Q25. How did information security evolve during the 1990s?

Answer:

- Computer networks became increasingly common.
    
- The Internet developed into a global network of networks.
    
- Network connections initially relied on de facto standards.
    
- Security initially received relatively little attention during early Internet expansion.
    
- Large corporations began integrating security into their organizational structures.
    

Information security began emerging as an independent professional discipline.

Keyword — De facto standard: A method or technology widely accepted through common usage rather than necessarily through formal standardization.

Q26. What major changes occurred in information security from 2000 onward?

Answer:

1. Millions of computer networks became interconnected through the Internet.
    
2. A system's security increasingly depended on other systems connected to it.
    
3. Cyberattacks became more frequent and sophisticated.
    
4. Financially motivated cybercriminal activity increased.
    
5. Theft, extortion, and government-sponsored cyberwarfare became significant concerns.
    

Q27. How have cyberattackers evolved over time?

Answer: Earlier attackers were often described as semiprofessional hackers who defaced websites for amusement.

The modern threat environment includes professional cybercriminals seeking financial profit and government-sponsored groups targeting military, governmental, and commercial systems.

Q28. What is the difference between computer security and information security?

|Computer security|Information security|
|---|---|
|Primarily protects computer systems and equipment.|Protects information and all supporting assets.|
|Originally emphasized physical and system-level protection.|Includes technical, administrative, and human protections.|
|Focuses largely on computing resources.|Covers policies, people, procedures, networks, software, hardware, and data.|

Important: Information security evolved from computer security and has a broader scope.

## SECTION C: The CIA Triad and Critical Characteristics of Information

Slides 17, 20–21 and 24

Q31. What is the CIA triad?

Answer: The CIA triad is a foundational information security model consisting of:

- C — Confidentiality
    
- I — Integrity
    
- A — Availability
    

It represents three fundamental security objectives.

Study illustration of the CIA triad (slide 21).

Q32. What is confidentiality?

Answer: Confidentiality means ensuring information is accessible only to individuals, systems, or processes authorized to access it.

Examples:

- Password-protected files.
    
- Encryption of private messages.
    
- Restricted access to employee salary records.
    

Q33. What is integrity?

Answer: Integrity means preserving information in a complete and unaltered condition, except when modifications are properly authorized.

Examples:

- Preventing unauthorized changes to examination scores.
    
- Using file hashes to detect alterations.
    
- Protecting financial transactions against tampering.
    

Q34. What is availability?

Answer: Availability means ensuring authorized users can access information and information systems when required.

Examples:

- Maintaining backup servers.
    
- Using backup power supplies.
    
- Restoring systems after failures.
    
- Protecting systems against denial-of-service attacks.
    

Q35. What are examples of violations of the CIA triad?

|Scenario|Security characteristic violated|
|---|---|
|A hacker reads confidential customer information.|Confidentiality|
|An attacker changes a student's examination grade.|Integrity|
|A server outage prevents employees from accessing their files.|Availability|

Q36. Why is the CIA triad considered insufficient by itself?

Answer: Although the CIA triad captures fundamental information security objectives, it does not fully represent every characteristic that makes information valuable.

The module therefore introduces four additional characteristics: accuracy, authenticity, utility, and possession.

Q37. What are the seven critical characteristics of information?

Answer:

1. Confidentiality
    
2. Integrity
    
3. Availability
    
4. Accuracy
    
5. Authenticity
    
6. Utility
    
7. Possession
    

Q38. What is accuracy in information security?

Answer: Accuracy is the degree to which information is correct, precise, and free from errors.

Example: A patient's medical record containing the correct blood group and medication dosage demonstrates accuracy.

Q39. What is authenticity?

Answer: Authenticity means that information is genuine and can be verified as originating from its claimed source.

Example: A digitally signed message can help verify that it was sent by the claimed sender.

Q40. What is utility?

Answer: Utility refers to the usefulness or suitability of information for its intended purpose.

Example: An encrypted backup might remain intact, but without the decryption key, the information may be unusable.

Q41. What is possession?

Answer: Possession refers to having control or custody of information.

Possession is distinct from confidentiality because someone may take information without being able to understand its contents.

Example: A thief steals an encrypted hard drive. The organization has lost possession of the information even if the thief cannot decrypt it.

Q42. What is the difference between integrity and accuracy?

Answer:

- Integrity: Information has not been improperly changed.
    
- Accuracy: Information correctly represents the facts.
    

Example: A student enters an incorrect date of birth into a registration system. The record may retain its integrity if nobody subsequently modifies it, but it is inaccurate.

Q43. What is the difference between authenticity and confidentiality?

Answer:

- Authenticity confirms that information or its claimed source is genuine.
    
- Confidentiality prevents unauthorized disclosure.
    

A message can be authentic but publicly readable, or confidential without the receiver being sure who sent it.

Q44. What is the difference between possession and confidentiality?

Answer: Possession concerns control of information, whereas confidentiality concerns unauthorized disclosure.

Example: A stolen encrypted USB drive represents a loss of possession even if confidentiality remains protected by strong encryption.

## SECTION D: Key Information Security Terminology

Slides 22–23

The module identifies the following important security concepts. These concise definitions and examples expand the listed terms for study purposes.

Q45. What is an asset?

Answer: An asset is anything of value to an organization that requires protection.

Examples: Customer databases, servers, software, financial records, intellectual property, and employees' expertise.

Q46. What is access?

Answer: Access is the ability of a user, program, or system to interact with or use a resource.

Example: An employee logs into a database to view authorized records.

Q47. What is a threat?

Answer: A threat is a potential cause of harm to an information asset or system.

Examples: Malware, fire, theft, flooding, or unauthorized access.

Q48. What is a vulnerability?

Answer: A vulnerability is a weakness in a system, process, configuration, or security measure that a threat could exploit.

Examples:

- Weak passwords.
    
- Outdated software.
    
- Unpatched operating systems.
    
- Poorly configured access permissions.
    

Q49. What is an attack?

Answer: An attack is an intentional act that attempts to compromise the security of an information asset.

Example: A cybercriminal attempts to gain access to a database using stolen login credentials.

Q50. What is an exploit?

Answer: An exploit is a technique, program, or method used to take advantage of a vulnerability.

Example: An attacker uses a known SQL injection vulnerability to access a database.

Q51. What is a threat agent?

Answer: A threat agent is an entity capable of carrying out an action that causes harm to an information system.

Examples: A malicious employee, external hacker, or cybercriminal organization.

Q52. What is a threat source?

Answer: A threat source is the origin of a potential threat.

Examples: A criminal group, natural disaster, equipment failure, or human error.

A threat source can be malicious or non-malicious.

Q53. What is a threat event?

Answer: A threat event is a specific occurrence or action that could cause a loss or security incident.

Examples: A ransomware infection, stolen laptop, or server-room fire.

Q54. What is risk?

Answer: Risk is the possibility of loss or damage arising when a threat exploits a vulnerability and affects an asset.

Risk assessment considers factors such as the likelihood of an event and the seriousness of its consequences.

Example: A server with unpatched software faces the risk of compromise by an attacker.

Q55. What is exposure?

Answer: Exposure is the condition in which an information asset is susceptible to potential loss because of an existing vulnerability or lack of sufficient protection.

Example: A database exposed directly to the Internet without adequate access controls.

Q56. What is loss?

Answer: Loss is the damage, reduction in value, or negative consequence suffered when a security incident affects an asset.

Examples: Financial loss, stolen information, destroyed files, or interrupted business operations.

Q57. What is a control, safeguard, or countermeasure?

Answer: A control is a measure implemented to prevent, detect, reduce, or respond to security risks.

Examples:

- Firewalls
    
- Antivirus software
    
- Security policies
    
- Employee training
    
- Access restrictions
    
- Backups
    

Q58. What is a security posture or protection profile?

Answer: An organization's security posture is its overall security condition, including its existing protections, vulnerabilities, and ability to withstand threats.

A protection profile describes security requirements or protections expected for a particular system or class of systems.

Example: An organization with strong authentication, updated software, tested backups, and incident response procedures is generally better protected than one without those safeguards.

Q59. What are subjects and objects in information security?

Answer:

- Subject: An active entity that requests access or performs an action.
    
- Object: A resource that is accessed, read, modified, or otherwise acted upon.
    

Example: When an employee opens a protected file, the employee is the subject and the file is the object.

Q60. How are assets, threats, vulnerabilities, exploits, attacks, and losses related?

Answer: These concepts describe how a security incident can happen.

Asset: Valuable customer database

Vulnerability: Application is susceptible to SQL injection

Threat agent: Cybercriminal

Exploit/Attack: Attacker exploits SQL injection

Loss: Customer information is stolen

The organization can reduce risk by implementing countermeasures such as input validation, access controls, and regular security updates.

## SECTION F: Components of an Information System

Slide 26

Q68. What is an information system (IS)?

Answer: An information system is the combination of hardware, software, data, people, procedures, and networks that work together to allow individuals or organizations to use information.

Q69. What are the six major components of an information system?

Answer:

|Component|Definition|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Hardware|Physical equipment used to process and store information.|Computers, servers, routers|
|Software|Programs and instructions that perform tasks.|Operating systems, applications|
|Data|Facts and records processed by a system.|Customer records|
|People|Individuals who use, manage, or maintain the system.|Employees, administrators|
|Procedures|Instructions and methods for using and managing the system.|Backup procedures|
|Networks|Communication systems linking devices and resources.|LAN, Internet|

Q70. Why must all six information system components be secured?

Answer: Each component has its own weaknesses and security requirements.

For example:

- Hardware can be stolen or damaged.
    
- Software can contain vulnerabilities.
    
- Data can be altered or disclosed.
    
- People can make mistakes.
    
- Procedures can be inadequate.
    
- Networks can be intercepted or attacked.
    

A weakness in one component can compromise the security of the entire system.

## SECTION G: Balancing Information Security and Access

Slide 27

Q71. Is perfect information security achievable?

Answer: No. Perfect information security is impossible.

The module emphasizes that security is an ongoing process rather than a goal that can be achieved permanently.

Q72. Why is information security described as a process rather than a goal?

Answer: Because technologies, users, organizational needs, vulnerabilities, and threats change continuously.

Security measures must therefore be evaluated, maintained, and improved over time.

Q73. What is meant by balancing information security and access?

Answer: It means implementing enough security to protect information against threats while still allowing authorized users reasonable access to the resources they need.

Q74. Why is excessive security sometimes a problem?

Answer: Excessively restrictive controls may prevent authorized users from accessing necessary information or completing their work efficiently.

Example: Requiring multiple lengthy approval processes before an employee can access an ordinary work document may unnecessarily delay operations.

Q75. What happens when access is prioritized without sufficient security?

Answer: Systems may become easier for unauthorized users to exploit, increasing the risk of information theft, modification, or destruction.

Key principle: Effective security requires a balance between protection and availability.

## SECTION H: Approaches to Implementing Information Security

Slides 28–30

Q76. What are the two main approaches to information security implementation?

Answer:

1. Bottom-up approach.
    
2. Top-down approach.
    

Q77. What is the bottom-up approach to information security?

Answer: The bottom-up approach is a grassroots effort in which technical personnel, such as system administrators, independently improve security within the systems they manage.

Example: A network administrator introduces stronger firewall rules and password requirements without a formal organization-wide security initiative.

Q78. What is the main advantage of the bottom-up approach?

Answer: It benefits from the technical expertise and practical experience of system administrators and other technical professionals.

Q79. Why does the bottom-up approach often fail?

Answer: According to the module, it commonly lacks:

- Sufficient participant support.
    
- Organizational staying power.
    

It may also lack the sustained management commitment needed to maintain a security program across the organization.

Q80. What is the top-down approach to information security?

Answer: The top-down approach is a security implementation strategy initiated and supported by senior management.

Management establishes:

1. Policies.
    
2. Procedures and processes.
    
3. Project goals.
    
4. Expected outcomes.
    
5. Accountability for security-related activities.
    

Q81. Which information security implementation approach is more successful?

Answer: The top-down approach is generally considered more successful because senior management provides authority, direction, accountability, and organizational support.

Q82. What is the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC)?

Answer: The Systems Development Life Cycle is a structured method for planning, developing, implementing, and maintaining information systems.

The module identifies a formal development strategy such as the SDLC as an important part of successful top-down security implementation.

Q83. What are the major differences between the top-down and bottom-up approaches?

|Top-down approach|Bottom-up approach|
|---|---|
|Initiated by senior management.|Initiated by technical personnel.|
|Organization-wide planning.|Individual or departmental initiatives.|
|Policies and goals are formally established.|Security measures may be introduced independently.|
|Provides stronger authority and accountability.|Relies heavily on technical expertise.|
|Generally more successful.|Often lacks lasting organizational support.|

## SECTION J: Data Responsibilities

Slides 37–39 and 45

Q102. What are the four categories of data responsibilities?

Answer:

1. Data owners
    
2. Data custodians
    
3. Data trustees
    
4. Data users
    

Q103. Who is a data owner?

Answer: A data owner is a senior management representative responsible for the security and appropriate use of a particular set of information.

Example: A finance director who is responsible for financial records.

Q104. Who is a data custodian?

Answer: A data custodian is responsible for maintaining, storing, and protecting information and the systems that process, transmit, and store it.

Example: A database administrator responsible for backups and secure database storage.

Q105. Who is a data trustee?

Answer: A data trustee is someone appointed by a data owner to oversee the management of a particular set of information and coordinate with data custodians concerning its storage, protection, and use.

Example: A departmental records manager appointed to oversee the handling of sensitive employee information.

Q106. Who is a data user?

Answer: A data user is an authorized individual who accesses or works with information to perform daily job responsibilities.

Example: An accountant who accesses authorized financial records to prepare reports.

Q107. What is the difference between a data owner, custodian, trustee, and user?

|Role|Main responsibility|
|---|---|
|Data owner|Ultimately responsible for the security and appropriate use of a particular dataset.|
|Data custodian|Maintains and protects the data and supporting infrastructure.|
|Data trustee|Oversees data management on behalf of the owner and coordinates with custodians.|
|Data user|Uses the information to perform authorized tasks.|

Q108. Who appoints data trustees?

Answer: Data owners.

Q109. Who coordinates with data custodians to oversee information storage, protection, and use?

Answer: Data trustees.

Q110. Who is responsible for the technical storage, maintenance, and protection of information?

Answer: Data custodians.

Q111. Why do data users have information security responsibilities?

Answer: Because their actions can either protect or compromise information.

For example, a data user who shares a password or falls for a phishing email could expose organizational resources to unauthorized access.

Answer: Protecting organizational information assets and reducing security risks.

Q115. What is the main interest of the information technology community?

Answer: Ensuring information systems and technological resources function properly and support organizational operations.

Q116. What is the main interest of general management?

Answer: Achieving organizational objectives and ensuring resources, including information and technology, are used effectively.

Q117. Why must the three communities of interest cooperate?

Answer: Information security requires a balance between business goals, technical capabilities, and appropriate protection.

For example, management may require a new online service, IT professionals develop and maintain it, and information security professionals determine how to protect its information.

## SECTION L: Information Security as an Art, Science, and Social Science

Slides 41–42

Q118. Is information security an art or a science?

Answer: Information security is described as a combination of art and science, with important elements of social science.

Q119. Why is information security considered an art?

Answer: Because information security problems do not always have universal solutions.

Security professionals must frequently use experience, judgment, creativity, and practical knowledge to design solutions suited to particular environments.

Example: Two organizations may need different security arrangements because their technology, employees, and business requirements differ.

Q120. What is meant by the term security artisan?

Answer: A security artisan is a skilled practitioner who relies on practical experience, technical expertise, and judgment when implementing security solutions.

The term reflects the idea that effective security work may require specialized craftsmanship rather than simply following fixed instructions.

Q121. Why is information security considered a science?

Answer: Because it involves technologies developed through scientific and engineering principles.

Computer systems operate according to defined technical conditions, and many vulnerabilities result from specific interactions between hardware and software.

Example: An engineer studies a software flaw, reproduces the vulnerability, and develops a patch to fix it.

Q122. Why is information security considered a social science?

Answer: Because it involves understanding how people interact with technological systems.

Human behavior affects security, whether intentionally or unintentionally.

Q123. Why are people important to information security?

Answer: People design, configure, manage, and use information systems.

Their actions can strengthen security or create vulnerabilities.

Examples:

- An employee uses a weak password.
    
- A worker opens a phishing email.
    
- An administrator misconfigures a server.
    
- A user accidentally shares sensitive information.
    

Q124. How can security administrators reduce risks caused by end users?

Answer: Through measures such as:

1. Security education and awareness.
    
2. Appropriate security policies.
    
3. User training.
    
4. Access restrictions.
    
5. Usable security procedures.
    
6. Monitoring and improvement.
    

The objective is to create security arrangements that are both effective and acceptable to the people who use them.

## SECTION P: Likely Short-Answer and Essay Questions

These are possible examination questions derived from the learning objectives and main topics, rather than questions explicitly set by the lecturer.

Q125. Explain the evolution of information security from World War II to the present.

Answer:

Information security developed through several historical stages:

1. World War II and early computing: Protection focused on equipment, classified documents, code-breaking systems, and physical facilities.
    
2. 1960s: Mainframes became more widely used, and research into networked communications led to ARPANET.
    
3. 1970s and 1980s: Security expanded to passwords, access controls, operating systems, and formal management policies. RAND Report R-609 was especially influential.
    
4. 1990s: The expansion of interconnected networks and the Internet created new vulnerabilities. Information security developed as a distinct professional discipline.
    
5. 2000 onward: Cybercrime, theft, extortion, and government-sponsored attacks increased the need for comprehensive organizational security.
    

Conclusion: Information security evolved from primarily physical computer protection into a broad discipline covering technology, information, people, policies, and organizational processes.

Q126. Discuss the CIA triad and explain why other information characteristics are also important.

Answer:

The CIA triad describes three foundational objectives of information security.

- Confidentiality: Preventing unauthorized disclosure of information.
    
- Integrity: Protecting information from unauthorized or improper modification.
    
- Availability: Ensuring information is accessible to authorized users when required.
    

Although these principles are fundamental, the module identifies four additional critical characteristics:

- Accuracy: Information must be correct.
    
- Authenticity: Information must be genuine.
    
- Utility: Information must be useful for its intended purpose.
    
- Possession: Information must remain under appropriate control.
    

Together, the seven characteristics provide a broader understanding of how information can retain its value and remain protected.

Q127. Explain why the top-down approach is preferred for information security implementation.

Answer:

The top-down approach is preferred because security initiatives require sustained organizational authority and support.

Senior management defines security goals, establishes policies and procedures, assigns accountability, and directs implementation.

Unlike bottom-up initiatives, which depend heavily on the efforts of individual technical workers, a top-down approach can align security activities across the entire organization.

Q128. Discuss the responsibilities of data owners, trustees, custodians, and users in information security.

Answer:

The four roles support effective information management through distinct responsibilities.

- Data owners: Senior managers responsible for the security and appropriate use of particular information assets.
    
- Data trustees: Individuals appointed by data owners to oversee information management and coordinate protection and use.
    
- Data custodians: Personnel responsible for storing, maintaining, and protecting the information and supporting infrastructure.
    
- Data users: Authorized individuals who access information to complete organizational tasks.
    

All four roles contribute to protecting organizational information.

Q129. Explain why information security can be considered an art, science, and social science.

Answer:

Information security combines three perspectives:

1. Art: Professionals must use experience, creativity, and judgment because security solutions cannot always follow universal rules.
    
2. Science: Security depends on engineering, computing principles, and an understanding of how hardware and software systems operate.
    
3. Social science: Human behavior influences security, so employee training, awareness, and organizational practices are essential.
    

Effective information security requires all three perspectives.

## SECTION A: Additional Cybersecurity Concepts

Source: Slides 2–9

Q1. What is cyberspace?

Answer: Cyberspace is the environment consisting of computer and communication technologies, the information they process or store, and the connections between them.

It includes:

- Computers, servers, and mobile devices.
    
- Communication networks and the Internet.
    
- Software applications and cloud services.
    
- Digital information and interconnected systems.
    

Example: Online banking involves customers' devices, the Internet, bank servers, applications, and transaction data. Together, these operate within cyberspace.

Q2. What is cybersecurity according to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU-T)?

Answer: Cybersecurity is the collection of tools, policies, security concepts, safeguards, guidelines, risk management approaches, training, technologies, and best practices used to protect cyberspace and organizational or user assets.

Cybersecurity protects:

- Connected computing devices and infrastructure.
    
- Applications and services.
    
- Telecommunications systems.
    
- Personnel and organizational assets.
    
- Stored and transmitted information.
    

Its fundamental objectives are confidentiality, integrity, and availability, with related properties such as authenticity and non-repudiation.

Q3. What is the difference between information security, network security, and cybersecurity?

|Concept|Main focus|
|---|---|
|Information security|Protecting information in any form.|
|Network security|Protecting networks and network services from unauthorized modification, destruction, or disclosure.|
|Cybersecurity|Protecting the wider interconnected digital environment, including systems, networks, services, and users.|

Example: Locking a cabinet containing confidential documents supports information security. Configuring a firewall supports network security. Protecting an organization against coordinated attacks across its cloud services and network supports cybersecurity.

Q4. What is accountability in cybersecurity?

Answer: Accountability is the ability to trace actions performed within a system to the specific individual or entity responsible.

Example: A system records which employee accessed, modified, or deleted a document, together with the time of the action.

Important distinction: Accountability identifies who performed an action, while authenticity establishes whether an entity or information source is genuine.

Q5. What is non-repudiation?

Answer: Non-repudiation is the assurance that participants in a communication or transaction cannot credibly deny their involvement.

It can involve evidence of:

- The identity of the sender.
    
- The origin of a message.
    
- The delivery or receipt of information.
    

Example: A properly implemented digital signature can provide evidence that a particular person signed an electronic agreement.

Q6. What is reliability in information security?

Answer: Reliability is the ability of a system to perform its intended functions correctly and consistently over time.

Example: A banking application processes transactions accurately without frequent crashes or unexpected failures.

Difference from availability: Availability concerns whether a system can be accessed when needed. Reliability concerns whether the system operates correctly and consistently.

Q7. What are the four major cybersecurity dilemmas?

Answer:

1. Scale and complexity of cyberspace: The enormous number of devices, networks, applications, and users makes security difficult.
    
2. Nature of the threat: Threats continually evolve and may originate from criminals, terrorists, hostile states, or other malicious actors. Legitimate data collection may also create privacy risks.
    
3. User needs versus security implementation: Users want convenient, powerful systems, but security restrictions may reduce convenience.
    
4. Difficulty estimating costs and benefits: Organizations may struggle to calculate the total damage from security breaches and the financial value of preventing them.
    

Example: An organization requires frequent complex password changes. Employees find the requirement inconvenient and start writing passwords on sticky notes, potentially weakening security.

Q8. Why do complex information systems create greater cybersecurity challenges?

Answer: Complex systems contain numerous interconnected components, users, and applications. Each may introduce vulnerabilities or require additional security controls.

The challenge increases as technology changes, new services are introduced, and societal expectations evolve.

Example: A company using cloud services, employee laptops, IoT sensors, and mobile applications must protect all these components and their connections.

Q9. Why is it difficult to estimate the benefits of cybersecurity investments?

Answer: Cybersecurity investments often prevent events that might otherwise occur. Because avoided attacks and their potential costs are difficult to measure, calculating the exact financial benefit of a security control can be challenging.

Example: A company spends money improving its firewall, but cannot know precisely how many attacks were prevented or what each successful attack would have cost.

Q26. What is digital forensics?

Q68. What is continuous vulnerability management?

Answer: Continuous vulnerability management is the ongoing process of identifying, evaluating, prioritizing, and correcting security weaknesses in organizational systems.

Example: An IT team regularly scans company servers for vulnerabilities and installs necessary security patches.

Q69. What are administrative privileges?

Answer: Administrative privileges are elevated permissions allowing a user or account to change system configurations, manage users, install software, or perform other restricted operations.

Example: A system administrator can create new accounts or modify server security settings.

Security principle: Administrative privileges should be tightly controlled because compromised privileged accounts can cause significant damage.

Q70. What is an audit log?

It may contain:

- Login attempts.
    
- File modifications.
    
- Permission changes.
    
- System errors.
    
- Administrative actions.
    

Example: Logs reveal that an employee account attempted to access a restricted server at midnight.

Q71. What is boundary defense?

Answer: Boundary defense involves protecting the points at which an organization's systems and networks connect to other networks or less-trusted environments.

Q72. What is the principle of need to know?

Answer: Need to know means providing access to information only to individuals who require it to perform their authorized responsibilities.

Example: A payroll employee can access salary records, while an employee in a department without payroll responsibilities cannot.

Q73. What is penetration testing?

Answer: Penetration testing is an authorized security assessment in which testers simulate attacks to identify vulnerabilities and determine whether they can be exploited.

Example: A company authorizes security testers to examine its website for exploitable vulnerabilities before attackers discover them.

Q74. What is a red team exercise?

Answer: A red team exercise is an authorized simulation of realistic attacks designed to evaluate an organization's detection, protection, and response capabilities.

Difference:

- Penetration testing generally focuses on identifying and exploiting vulnerabilities within an agreed scope.
    
- Red teaming generally evaluates broader defensive capabilities against realistic adversary objectives.

## SECTION A: Why Organizations Need Information Security

Source: Slides 2–9

Q1. What is the primary mission of an information security program?

Answer: The primary mission is to ensure that an organization's information assets, including information and the systems that store or process it, remain safe and useful.

An information security program must address both existing and potential security problems.

Q2. What are the four major functions of information security in an organization?

Answer:

1. Protecting the organization's ability to function: Ensuring that security incidents do not prevent business operations.
2. Protecting organizational data and information: Safeguarding information collected, processed, stored, and transmitted.
3. Enabling safe operation of applications: Ensuring software applications operate securely within the organization's IT environment.
4. Safeguarding technology assets: Protecting hardware, software, networks, and other technological resources.

Example: An online bank uses security measures to ensure customers can perform transactions, customer records remain protected, banking applications function securely, and its servers remain operational.

Exam tip: Memorize all four functions. The lecture specifically identifies them as the primary functions of information security.

Q3. What does the principle "Business Needs First" mean?

Answer: Information security must support the organization's objectives rather than prevent it from operating.

According to the lecture, when security requirements conflict with business needs, the organization must consider business priorities because information has value through its contribution to business activities.

This does not mean ignoring security. It means balancing effective protection with organizational functionality.

Example: A hospital must protect patient records while ensuring doctors can access critical information during emergencies.

Q4. Why should information security be viewed as a management issue rather than only a technical issue?

| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |

The three information states were already introduced in Module 01 through the McCumber Cube. Module 02 emphasizes their importance to protecting organizational data.

Q6. Why must information security solutions change as an organization grows?

Q37. What are the major password attack methods?

Answer:

1. Password cracking.
2. Brute-force attacks.
3. Dictionary attacks.
4. Rainbow table attacks.
5. Social engineering.

Q38. What is password cracking?

Answer: Password cracking is the process of attempting to discover or recover passwords, often by guessing candidates or analyzing stored password hashes.

Example: An attacker attempts to recover a password from a leaked password database.

Q39. What is a brute-force password attack?

Answer: A brute-force attack systematically tries possible password combinations until a correct one is discovered.

Example: Testing combinations of letters, numbers, and symbols against a password hash.

Q40. What is a dictionary attack?

Answer: A dictionary attack attempts to discover passwords using a list of common words, known passwords, and frequently used combinations.

Example: Testing passwords such as `password123`, `welcome123`, and `admin123`.

Q41. What is a rainbow table attack?

Answer: A rainbow table attack uses precomputed data to help recover passwords from certain password hashes.

It can be effective against inadequately protected password hashes, particularly unsalted hashes.

Q42. What is the difference between brute-force, dictionary, and rainbow table attacks?

| Attack        | Main technique                                                    |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Brute force   | Systematically tries possible combinations.                       |
| Dictionary    | Tries likely passwords from prepared lists.                       |
| Rainbow table | Uses precomputed hash-related data to speed up password recovery. |

Q43. How do password length and character variety affect password strength?

Answer: Passwords become more resistant to exhaustive guessing when the number of possible combinations increases.

The lecture illustrates this using two password groups:

- Case-insensitive passwords using a standard alphabet without numbers or symbols.
- Case-sensitive passwords using letters, numbers, and 20 special characters.

The number of possible passwords is calculated as:

\\[ N=C^L \\]

Where:

- \\(N\\) = Number of possible combinations.
- \\(C\\) = Number of characters available.
- \\(L\\) = Password length.

Example: Using 26 lowercase letters in an 8-character password produces:

\\[ 26^8=208,827,064,576 \\]

possible combinations.

Exam point: Increasing password length greatly increases the potential password search space.

Q44. What do the lecture's password strength tables demonstrate?

Answer: The tables show that longer passwords and larger character sets produce exponentially more possible combinations.

For an 8-character password, the lecture calculates:

- Approximately 208.8 billion possibilities using 26 letters.
- Approximately 2.04 quadrillion possibilities using the larger character set.

The lecture's estimated cracking times are illustrative and depend on the computing speed and attack assumptions used.

## SECTION J: Malware and Software Attacks

Q67. What is malware?

Answer: Malware stands for malicious software.

Q68. What is a zero-day attack?

Answer: In the lecture, a zero-day attack is described as an attack using malware that is not yet recognized by antimalware companies.

Q69. What is a computer virus?

Answer: A computer virus is malicious code that attaches to an existing program or file and spreads when the infected program or file is executed.

Example: An infected executable file spreads malicious code when users open it.

Q70. What is a computer worm?

Answer: A worm is malware capable of replicating and spreading between systems without needing to attach itself to another program.

Worms may consume network bandwidth, memory, and storage resources.

Example: A worm exploits vulnerable network services to spread across multiple computers.

Q71. What is a Trojan horse?

Answer: A Trojan horse is malicious software disguised as a legitimate, useful, or desirable application.

Example: A program advertised as a free video player secretly installs malicious software.

Q72. What is the difference between a virus, worm, and Trojan horse?

| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |

Q73. What is polymorphic malware?

Answer: Polymorphic malware is malicious software that changes aspects of its code or appearance as it spreads or executes, making detection more difficult.

Example: Malware repeatedly changes its detectable code patterns while preserving its malicious behavior.

Q74. What is a virus or worm hoax?

Answer: A virus or worm hoax is a false warning about nonexistent or misleadingly described malware.

It may cause employees to waste time forwarding warnings or performing unnecessary actions.

Q75. What is a backdoor?

Answer: A backdoor is a hidden or alternative access mechanism that bypasses normal authentication or security controls.

Example: Malware creates a secret method of remotely accessing an infected computer.

Q76. What is a Denial-of-Service (DoS) attack?

Answer: A DoS attack attempts to make a system or service unavailable by overwhelming it with requests or otherwise exhausting its resources.

Example: A website receives so many malicious requests that legitimate visitors cannot load its pages.

Q77. What is a Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack?

Answer: A DDoS attack involves coordinated traffic or requests from many different systems directed at a single target.

DoS

One attacking source, or a concentrated source of disruptive traffic.

DDoS

Multiple distributed sources attack the same target.

Q79. What is mail bombing?

Answer: Mail bombing is an attack in which a large number of email messages are sent to a target to overwhelm the recipient's inbox or email infrastructure.

It can function as a denial-of-service attack.

Q80. What is spam?

Answer: Spam is unsolicited bulk messaging, commonly associated with unwanted commercial email.

The lecture notes that spam may be more of a nuisance than a direct attack, although it can also distribute malicious content.

Q81. What is a packet sniffer?

Answer: A packet sniffer is software or hardware used to monitor network traffic.

It can be used legitimately for network administration or maliciously to collect sensitive information.

Example: A network administrator captures packets to troubleshoot connectivity problems. An attacker might use similar technology to inspect unprotected communications.

Q82. What is spoofing?

Answer: Spoofing involves impersonating a trusted identity, system, address, or source.

The lecture specifically describes attackers assuming trusted IP addresses.

Example: An attacker creates network packets containing a forged source IP address.

Q83. What is pharming?

Answer: Pharming is an attack that redirects users from a legitimate website destination to a fraudulent website.

Example: A user enters a bank's correct website address but is redirected to an imitation banking site because of manipulated name resolution or related settings.

Q84. What is a Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attack?

Answer: A Man-in-the-Middle attack occurs when an attacker positions themselves between communicating parties and intercepts, monitors, or alters their communications.

Example: An attacker intercepts unprotected network communications between a user and a web service.

## SECTION L: Technical Hardware and Software Failures

Q94. What is a technical hardware failure?

Answer: A technical hardware failure occurs when physical equipment fails to perform as expected due to defects, aging, or other hardware problems.

Failures may be:

- Terminal: The equipment stops functioning and requires repair or replacement.
- Intermittent: The equipment malfunctions occasionally but may continue operating between failures.

## SECTION M: The Deadly Sins in Software Security

Source: Slides 68–70

This is a major section of Module 02 because the lecture explicitly lists software development weaknesses that may lead to security vulnerabilities.

Q101. What are the Deadly Sins in Software Security?

Answer: They are common software design, programming, configuration, and implementation mistakes that can create exploitable security weaknesses.

The lecture provides several categories of problems, including SQL injection, cross-site scripting, buffer overflows, insecure privilege management, and incorrect use of cryptography.

Q102. What is SQL injection?

Answer: SQL injection occurs when untrusted input is improperly incorporated into a database query, allowing the input to alter the intended SQL command.

Example: A poorly designed login application directly combines user input with an SQL query, allowing malicious input to change its behavior.

Q103. What is Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)?

Answer: XSS is a vulnerability that allows malicious scripts to run in another user's browser within the context of a vulnerable website.

Example: A website displays untrusted user comments as executable page content, allowing an attacker to inject malicious JavaScript.

Q104. What is Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF or XSRF)?

Answer: CSRF is an attack that tricks a user's browser into submitting an unintended request to a website where the user is already authenticated.

Example: A malicious website causes a logged-in user to submit an unwanted account change to another service.

Q105. What is HTTP response splitting?

Answer: HTTP response splitting occurs when untrusted input improperly influences HTTP response headers, allowing an attacker to alter how a response is interpreted.

Q106. What are magic URLs and hidden forms?

Answer: In the lecture's security context:

- Magic URLs: URLs containing special values or tokens that may control access or application behavior. They become dangerous when guessable, exposed, or improperly validated.
- Hidden form fields: Form values not visibly displayed to the user but still present in the page and potentially modifiable.

Example: An application trusts a hidden field containing a product price without validating it on the server.

Q107. What is a buffer overrun or buffer overflow?

Answer: A buffer overflow occurs when a program writes more data into a memory buffer than it can safely hold.

This can corrupt memory, crash programs, or potentially allow unintended code execution.

Q108. What is a format string vulnerability?

Answer: A format string vulnerability occurs when untrusted input is treated as a formatting instruction rather than ordinary data.

It may allow unintended memory access or other unsafe operations.

Q109. What are integer overflow and underflow vulnerabilities?

Answer:

- Integer overflow: A calculation produces a number greater than the maximum value supported by its data type.
- Integer underflow: A calculation goes below the supported minimum value, or below zero for certain unsigned operations.

These conditions may cause incorrect calculations or unexpected program behavior.

Q110. What are C++ catastrophes?

Answer: In the lecture's terminology, these refer to serious programming problems associated with unsafe C++ coding practices, particularly those involving memory management and object handling.

Examples may include use-after-free errors and improper pointer operations.

Q111. What is command injection?

Answer: Command injection occurs when an application improperly uses untrusted input in an operating system command.

This may allow unintended commands to execute.

Q112. What is improper exception handling?

Answer: Improper exception handling occurs when software fails to manage unexpected conditions safely.

An application may crash, reveal information, or enter an insecure state.

Q113. What is information leakage?

Answer: Information leakage is the unintended exposure of information to unauthorized individuals or systems.

Example: A web application displays detailed internal error messages that reveal database structure or system configuration.

Q114. What is a race condition?

Answer: A race condition occurs when a program's behavior depends on the timing or order of concurrent operations.

Example: Two processes simultaneously modify the same account balance without proper synchronization, producing an incorrect result.

Q115. Why is poor software usability a security problem?

Answer: Poor usability may encourage users to make mistakes or bypass security protections.

Example: A confusing permission interface causes an employee to accidentally share a confidential file publicly.

Q116. Why is difficulty updating software a security weakness?

Answer: Software that cannot be patched or updated easily may remain vulnerable to known security problems.

Q117. What is excessive privilege in software execution?

Answer: Excessive privilege occurs when software runs with more permissions than it requires.

Example: A simple text-processing application operates with full administrator privileges, increasing the potential damage if compromised.

Q118. What is the principle of least privilege?

Answer: The principle of least privilege states that users and programs should receive only the permissions necessary to perform their authorized tasks.

It helps reduce the effects of security failures and compromised accounts.

Q119. What is failure to protect stored data?

Answer: It occurs when applications do not adequately safeguard data kept in databases, files, or other storage systems.

Example: Sensitive customer information is stored without appropriate access restrictions.

Q120. What are the sins of mobile code?

Answer: They are security problems associated with code obtained from external sources or transferred between systems and executed in a different environment.

Example: An application executes untrusted downloaded code without appropriate security restrictions.

Q121. What is weak random number generation?

Answer: Weak random number generation occurs when software produces values that are insufficiently unpredictable for security purposes.

Example: Predictable security tokens may allow attackers to guess authentication or session values.

Q122. What does using cryptography incorrectly mean?

Answer: It means implementing or configuring encryption and other cryptographic mechanisms in a way that undermines their security.

Example: An application encrypts sensitive information but stores the encryption key in a publicly accessible location.

Q123. What is failure to protect network traffic?

Answer: It occurs when applications transmit sensitive information without sufficient protection against interception or tampering.

Example: A website sends login credentials over an unencrypted connection.

Q124. What is PKI?

Answer: PKI stands for Public Key Infrastructure.

It is a framework of technologies, certificates, policies, and processes used to manage public-key cryptography and digital identities.

Q125. What is SSL?

Answer: SSL stands for Secure Sockets Layer.

It is an older protocol family for protecting network communications. Modern systems generally use its successor, TLS (Transport Layer Security).

The lecture identifies incorrect use of PKI, particularly SSL, as a software security problem.

Q126. What is trusting network name resolution?

Answer: It refers to relying on network name resolution information without sufficient validation.

Example: An application trusts a manipulated DNS response and connects to an unintended server.

Keyword — DNS: Domain Name System; a system that translates domain names into information needed to locate network services, commonly including IP addresses.

Q127. What is change control in software security?

Answer: Change control is a structured process used to review, authorize, document, test, and manage modifications to software or systems.

Example: A company requires testing and management approval before deploying changes to a production payment application.

Q128. Why is neglecting change control a security risk?

Answer: Unauthorized or poorly tested changes may introduce new vulnerabilities, disrupt services, or weaken existing protections.

## SECTION Q: Likely Essay and Short-Answer Questions

Q133. Explain the 12 categories of information security threats, giving examples.

Answer: Information security threats fall into 12 categories: intellectual property compromise, quality-of-service deviation, espionage or trespass, forces of nature, human error, information extortion, sabotage or vandalism, software attacks, hardware failures, software failures, technological obsolescence, and theft.

They can affect organizational confidentiality, integrity, availability, operations, finances, and reputation. Their causes may be deliberate, accidental, technological, or environmental.

Q134. Discuss the importance of human behavior in information security.

Answer: Human behavior is important because employees interact with information and systems during normal operations. Mistakes resulting from poor training, incorrect assumptions, or carelessness can cause information disclosure, deletion, modification, and other security incidents.

Attackers may also exploit trust through social engineering, phishing, and business email compromise. Organizations can reduce these risks through awareness, training, and appropriate security controls.

Q135. Compare viruses, worms, Trojan horses, and ransomware.

Answer: Viruses attach to files or programs and spread when infected content executes. Worms can reproduce and spread independently. Trojan horses disguise themselves as legitimate software. Ransomware denies access to data or systems and demands payment.

These categories are not always mutually exclusive: a malicious program can exhibit characteristics of more than one type.

Q136. Explain why software development errors create information security risks.

Answer: Software vulnerabilities may arise from insecure input handling, memory management errors, poor authentication, excessive privileges, improper cryptography, and other development mistakes.

Attackers may exploit these weaknesses to access confidential information, alter data, or disrupt system availability.

Security testing, appropriate design practices, updates, change control, and secure programming help reduce these risks.

Q137. Explain the importance of quality of service to information security.

Answer: Organizations depend on external services such as Internet connectivity, electricity, and telecommunications.

Service failures can interrupt access to information and prevent important business functions. Service Level Agreements, power protection systems, backup arrangements, and contingency planning help organizations manage these threats.

Q138. Explain why technological obsolescence is dangerous to organizations.

Answer: Technological obsolescence occurs when hardware or software becomes outdated or unsupported. This may create reliability problems, compatibility issues, and security weaknesses.

Organizations should assess existing technology, plan upgrades, and replace systems that no longer meet operational or security requirements.

1. Independent ISSPs: Separate documents covering individual security issues.
    
2. Comprehensive ISSP: One document containing policies for all major issues.
    
3. Modular ISSP: A unified policy structure containing separate sections or modules for specific issues.
    

Q35. What are the differences between the three ISSP approaches?

|Approach|Advantage or characteristic|
|---|---|
|Independent|Each document concentrates on one issue and can be maintained separately.|
|Comprehensive|Provides a single document covering numerous issues.|
|Modular|Combines consistent administration with the flexibility to maintain issue-specific requirements.|

Example: A company using the modular approach might maintain separate email, Internet-use, and remote-access policy sections within a coordinated policy system.

Q36. What are the seven major components of an ISSP?

|No.|Component|What it covers|
|---|---|---|
|1|Statement of policy|Scope, applicability, technology definition, and responsibilities.|

Q37. What is meant by authorized access and equipment usage in an ISSP?

Answer: It defines who may use an organization's equipment or systems and how they are permitted to use them.

It addresses access rights, responsible use, and privacy protection.

Example: Employees may use corporate computers for authorized business purposes but cannot provide unauthorized individuals with access.

Q38. What is prohibited use of equipment?

Answer: Prohibited use refers to activities that an organization explicitly forbids when using its technology resources.

Examples include criminal activity, harassment, copyright violations, and actions that disrupt organizational systems.

Q39. Why must an ISSP define penalties for violations?

Answer: To establish accountability and clarify the consequences of failing to follow security requirements.

Example: An employee who intentionally shares confidential information may face disciplinary measures under organizational policy.

Q40. What is a Systems-Specific Security Policy (SysSP)?

Answer: A SysSP is a policy containing security requirements or instructions for configuring, managing, and maintaining a particular information system.

It often functions as a technical standard or procedure.

Example: A server security policy specifies permitted network connections, administrative settings, and access restrictions.

Q41. What are the two main forms of SysSPs?

Answer:

1. Managerial guidance: Management-defined directions and expectations for securing a specific system.
    
2. Technical specifications: Detailed technical rules used to configure or operate security mechanisms.
    

A combined SysSP may include both.

Q42. What is an Access Control List (ACL)?

Answer: An ACL is a collection of rules specifying which users, computers, or other entities may access particular resources.

The lecture explains that an ACL can restrict access based on a user, computer, time, duration, or file.

Example: A file server ACL allows members of the finance department to read payroll reports while denying access to other employees.

Q43. What is a configuration rule policy?

Answer: It is a policy that defines how a security system should respond to particular information, traffic, or events.

Example: A firewall rule specifies that incoming connections to an unauthorized network port must be blocked.

Q44. How do EISP, ISSP, and SysSP differ?

|EISP|ISSP|SysSP|
|---|---|---|
|Organization-wide direction.|Specific security issue.|Specific information system.|
|Strategic level.|Issue or technology-use level.|Configuration and operational level.|
|Usually executive-oriented.|Designed for affected employees and managers.|Often designed for administrators and technical personnel.|
|Example: Enterprise security policy.|Example: Email usage policy.|Example: Database access configuration policy.|

Q45. What are the six requirements for implementing an effective and legally defensible security policy?

Answer:

1. Development: The policy must use industry-accepted practices and receive formal management approval.
    
2. Dissemination: The policy must be distributed through appropriate communication methods.
    
3. Reading: Employees must review the policy.

Q47. What is the difference between reading, comprehension, and compliance?

Answer:

- Reading: The employee has reviewed the policy.
    
- Comprehension: The employee understands what the policy requires.
    
- Compliance: The employee agrees to and follows the policy requirements.
    

Example: An employee reads a password policy, understands the requirements, and then follows them when creating an account password.

Q48. Why must policies be enforced uniformly?

Answer: Uniform enforcement helps make security expectations consistent and supports fair, defensible application of organizational rules.

Example: Security requirements should apply to managers and ordinary employees according to their assigned responsibilities, rather than being arbitrarily ignored for particular individuals.

Q49. What is policy management?

Q50. What five elements are necessary for effective policy management?

1. A responsible policy manager or administrator.
    
2. A schedule for reviewing policies.
    
3. Established review procedures and practices.
    
4. Policy dates and revision dates.
    
5. Automated policy management.
    

Q51. What is automated policy management?

Answer: Automated policy management uses software and supporting processes to distribute, maintain, track, and administer policies.

Q90. What are spheres of security?

Answer: Spheres of security are layers or areas of protection surrounding an organization's information and systems.

The lecture uses them to illustrate how different security measures protect information against threats from outside and inside the organization.

Q91. What does the spheres of security diagram on slide 55 illustrate?

Answer: The diagram shows information at the center of several protective layers.

Q92. What is defense in depth?

Answer: Defense in depth is the implementation of security through multiple layers of safeguards and controls.

Its purpose is to provide protection even when an individual security measure is bypassed or fails.

Example: An organization protects its servers using perimeter firewalls, restricted network access, intrusion detection, strong authentication, encryption, and regular security monitoring.

Q93. Why is defense in depth important?

Answer: No single security control can provide complete protection.

Multiple layers reduce dependence on one safeguard and can limit the consequences of a control failure.

Q94. What is a security perimeter?

Answer: A security perimeter is a boundary separating protected internal systems from outside or less-trusted environments.

Example: A firewall-controlled boundary separates an organization's internal network from the public Internet.

Q95. What is the main limitation of a security perimeter?

Answer: A security perimeter primarily protects against threats crossing the boundary.

The lecture emphasizes that it does not automatically protect against internal attacks by employees or on-site physical threats.

Q96. What is a security domain?

Answer: A security domain is a logical or physical grouping of systems or resources that share common security requirements or levels of trust.

Example: An organization's payroll servers operate in a separate security domain from ordinary employee computers.

Q97. What is a Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)?

Answer: A DMZ is a network area that separates public-facing systems from an organization's more trusted internal network.

Example: A company places its public web server in a DMZ while keeping its internal database servers on a more restricted network.

## SECTION M: Likely Short-Answer and Essay Questions

Q106. Discuss the Six Ps of Information Security Management.

Answer: The Six Ps are Planning, Policy, Programs, Protection, People, and Project Management.

Planning establishes the direction of security activities. Policy defines organizational requirements. Programs coordinate ongoing security initiatives. Protection implements safeguards. People management ensures responsibilities and skills are properly managed. Project management oversees specific security initiatives.

Together, these functions help organizations manage security systematically.

Q107. Discuss the importance of information security governance and explain its five major goals.

Answer: Information security governance ensures that the organization's information security activities are directed and overseen by senior leadership.

Its five major goals are strategic alignment, risk management, resource management, performance measurement, and value delivery.

These goals help the organization manage information security risks while supporting business objectives and using resources effectively.

Q108. Explain the differences between EISP, ISSP, and SysSP.

Answer: An EISP establishes the organization's overall information security direction. An ISSP defines rules for specific security issues, such as email usage. A SysSP establishes security requirements for particular systems and may contain technical configuration instructions.

The three policy types complement one another by providing organization-wide, issue-level, and system-level guidance.

Q109. Explain how organizations can develop and implement effective security policies.

Answer: Organizations must develop security policies using accepted practices and obtain management approval.

Policies must then be disseminated, read, understood, formally accepted, and uniformly enforced.

They must also be maintained through regular reviews, assigned ownership, revision tracking, and appropriate policy management procedures.

Q110. Compare security education, security training, and security awareness.

Answer: Security awareness teaches employees to recognize basic threats and understand expected behavior. Training teaches practical skills for responding to threats. Education provides deeper theoretical understanding and supports long-term security improvement.

These three components form SETA and help reduce accidental security breaches while improving organizational security knowledge.

Q111. Explain the relationship between an information security framework and a security blueprint.

Answer: A security framework provides a recognized structure or specification for designing and selecting controls.

A security blueprint translates that structure into an organization's specific implementation plan, identifying which controls will be introduced, the tasks required, and their sequence.

The framework provides guidance, while the blueprint organizes actual implementation.

Q112. Explain defense in depth and describe why it is necessary.

Answer: Defense in depth is the use of several layers of security controls to protect information assets.

The approach reduces dependence on any single safeguard. If one control fails, other controls may continue to protect important resources.

Examples include firewalls, authentication, intrusion detection, access controls, encryption, monitoring, and employee training.

Q113. Explain the differences between management, operational, and technical security controls.

Answer: Management controls establish the direction and scope of security. Operational controls address personnel, physical, and other day-to-day security activities. Technical controls implement safeguards through systems and technologies.

All three categories are necessary for a comprehensive security program.
