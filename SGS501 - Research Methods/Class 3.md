## Purpose and Benefits of a Literature Review

**Q: What is the primary function of a scientific literature review?** A: The primary function is to collect and examine the state of current knowledge in a field by reviewing the work of scholars and researchers whose work has been recognized as valuable.

  

**Q: What are the three main purposes of conducting a literature review?** A: It establishes context for your work by showing what has been done in the area, exposes the gap in current knowledge regarding your research problem, and shows supervisors that you have done your research.

  

**Q: How does a literature review benefit the design of a new study?** A: It helps develop insights on the design of your own study by showing what has and has not been previously successful, and it may reveal conceptual insights or suggest possible hypotheses.

  

**Q: How does a literature review guide a researcher's methodology?** A: It provides ideas and direction for handling problems encountered, selecting techniques, finding sources of data, and discovering novel approaches for the research.

  

## The Research Process and Literature Sources

**Q: What are the primary steps of the research process cycle?** A: The steps involve identifying a question or problem, formulating objectives, planning and designing the research, generating research results, and interpreting results to derive conclusions.

  

**Q: What are the components of a conceptual model for a research plan?** A: A conceptual model includes the problem statement, objective(s)/targets, and methodology, all of which are supported and informed by a literature survey.

  

**Q: How do different literature sources rank in terms of reliability and newness?** A: Encyclopedias, monographs, and textbooks offer high reliability but low newness. Review papers and original journal papers fall in the middle, while other original papers (conferences, symposia) and reports offer low reliability but high newness.

  

**Q: What type of sources should be prioritized when conducting a literature review?** A: You should include "scientific literature" that has passed through "peer review", such as professional journals, formal research reports, university-affiliated bulletins, and monographs. You should also start reading the most recent publications first, as they focus on current knowledge and include references to earlier research.

  

## Conducting and Writing the Literature Review

**Q: What is the estimated workload for papers in a Master's or PhD thesis?** A: For a Master's (or PhD) thesis, you can expect to find 600 (2000) papers, read the abstracts of 300 (1000), download 150 (500), fully digest 25 (100), and include 75 (150) in your bibliography.

  

**Q: What are the three main steps to performing a literature review?** A: The steps are organizing (developing a framework and associating papers with specific knowledge areas), summarizing (identifying each article's contribution, purpose, and claims), and evaluating (assessing gaps, new context applications, or disagreements).

  

**Q: What rules must be followed when writing the actual literature review?** A: You must organize the review as a coherent synthesis rather than an unconnected list of summaries. You should not quote directly, use figures unless mandatory, or reference a source of an idea without having actually read it.

  

**Q: What is the purpose of Reference Management Software?** A: It allows researchers to link to and correctly cite referenced works, create proper citations in a desired style (such as APA or MLA), and automate the building of bibliographies, footnotes, or reference sections.

  

## Use of Generative AI in Research

**Q: What are the appropriate "assistant role" uses for Generative AI in research?** A: Generative AI can be used to discover semantic connections, brainstorm search terms, group articles by theme, build comparison tables, and refine language.

  

**Q: What are the strictly inappropriate "replacement role" uses of Generative AI?** A: AI should not evaluate study quality, generate text from unread papers, or synthesize core theoretical arguments, because it cannot replace human critical thinking or accountability.

  

**Q: What are the main risks associated with using Generative AI for research?** A: The key risks include hallucinated and fake references (invented citations or false data), biases and omissions (inheriting geographic/linguistic biases or failing to catch retracted papers), and privacy/confidentiality breaches when uploading unpublished or sensitive data to public platforms.

  

**Q: What are the guidelines regarding AI authorship and disclosure?** A: AI tools cannot be listed as authors because they lack legal status and cannot assume academic responsibility. Researchers must transparently report how and where AI was used, specifying the tool name, version, date, and specific prompts or workflows.
